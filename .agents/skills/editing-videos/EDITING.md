## Editing videos (taste)

A good edit makes the video's point as clearly as possible, for the people who will watch it,
where they will watch it. It isn't a house style: a two-hour talk, a podcast, a tutorial and a
30-second short each need a different edit. Work out which one this is before you cut anything.

For a small, specific request (fix a title, cut this sentence, swap this shot), make that change
well and leave the rest alone. Use this guide for *how*; don't re-edit what nobody asked about.

### Work with what's set up

Use only the tools and services that are already working where you are: the editor, what's
installed on this computer, and accounts or API keys that are already set up. Never sign up for
anything, ask for or paste a key, install something paid, or send the person's footage to an
outside service just to follow this guide. When a step would need a service that isn't set up
(stock footage or music, generated images, video or music, cloud transcription), skip it, do the best edit with
what's there, and at the end say what you skipped and what setting it up would add. A missing
service is never a reason to stop, and never a reason to lower the bar on the rest.

### 1. Read the video first

Read the whole transcript (and the brief, notes and markers) before changing anything, then
write your read in two to four sentences:

- **What it is**: someone teaching, a talk to a room, a conversation, a demo on a screen, a
  story, an announcement, a performance, a mix.
- **Who watches it, and where**: a phone feed, a TV, a podcast app, a website, a room.
- **The point**: in one sentence, what a viewer should know, feel or do at the end.
- **What carries it**: the words, a face, the screen, an action, music.
- **Its natural length**: what it takes to make the point, not a target to hit.

For example: "A 40-minute talk given to a live room, for people who weren't there, watched on a
TV or laptop. The point is the closing challenge. The words carry it, and the room's reactions
matter. Keep its pace; cut the housekeeping and the false starts."

Ask when the answer would change the edit and the content can't settle it: who it's for, where
it's going, a length it must fit, what has to stay, what's off limits. Ask few questions, make
them specific, and give your best guess with each. When you can't ask (you get one answer),
edit from your best read and say what you assumed. The person's words and the brief always win
over your read.

### 2. Set the pace from the content

Pace follows attention, not a clock: move on when the viewer has what the moment gives them.
Pick the row that fits each stretch; one video can change pace (a fast opening, a slow story).

| The stretch is… | Pauses kept | Picture changes | Captions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Made to stop a scroll (a short, a teaser) | 0.1–0.2 s; none at the start | every 1–3 s, each with a reason | 1–3 words at a time |
| Explaining or talking with someone | 0.3–0.5 s; longer before a key line | when there's something new to show; otherwise stay | 3–5 words |
| Speaking to a room, a story, something that should land | up to 1–2 s where the silence means something; keep the room's laughs and reactions | rarely; hold on the person | 4–6 words, or subtitles |
| Doing something on a screen | the screen sets it: cut the waiting (loading, typing), keep the result long enough to see | follow the action | 3–5 words |

Automatic silence and filler removal is a first pass. Put back the pauses that carry meaning,
and don't tighten a reflective moment to a feed's pace.

### 3. Shape it

- **Start where it becomes worth watching.** Cut the pre-roll, mic checks and housekeeping the
  audience doesn't need. Whatever the title promises shows up early: in the first 1–3 s of a
  short, the first 30 s of a long video.
- **A cold open** comes only from the video's own strongest moment, and stays true to what was
  meant in context.
- **One main thread.** If the opening raises a question, answer it. Pay off before any call to
  action; "comment to find out" is never the only answer.
- **Shorter isn't the goal; clearer is.** Cut tangents, repeats and failed takes. Keep what
  builds trust: humour, context, the room.
- **Chapters** go where the video really turns: a new point, a story, a demo, questions.
- **End when it's done.** After the last point lands, out briskly. Never fade the last word.

### 4. Cutting

- **Every cut has a reason**: it takes out dead time or a mistake, changes what we learn, or
  follows an action.
- **Cut between phrases** (what's said in one breath), in silence, never inside a word. Leave a
  breath before the next word: about 0.1–0.2 s, a little more after the end of a sentence.
- **Retakes**: keep the last clean take of a line. Join two takes only where both sides are
  silent; if they run together, keep one whole take. Saying something twice on purpose (a
  refrain, a list, emphasis) isn't a mistake. Detectors miss retakes said in other words, so
  read the transcript yourself; finding nothing to cut is a fine answer.
- **Fillers** go only where there's silence on both sides and the line reads naturally without
  them. Never speed up a voice to save time.
- **Jump cuts** on one camera: cover them with another angle, B-roll, or a punch-in that clearly
  changes the size (15% or more). Fast formats can leave them bare; slow ones shouldn't.
- **Sound leads and lingers**: let the next voice start under the end of a shot (a J-cut), or a
  voice carry over into the next shot (an L-cut), so cuts between people and places feel natural.
- **Several cameras**: the picture follows whoever is talking, holds reactions that matter, and
  never cuts to the same framing mid-sentence.
- **Zooms and punch-ins** need a reason (emphasis, a detail to see). Never across a cut, and not
  on every line.

### 5. B-roll and visuals

B-roll shows what's being said. It isn't decoration, and there's no quota: when the person is
the point (an emotional beat, a joke, a promise to camera), leave them on screen.

- **Anchor it to the word that names it.** It comes in at most 0.5 s before that word and stays
  until the idea is finished, long enough to take in (about 1.5 s or more).
- **Where it comes from, in order:**
  1. the video's own footage: other camera angles, screen recordings, earlier recordings, files
     the person handed over;
  2. real footage from a library with a clear license, if one is already set up;
  3. a diagram or graphic built from what was actually said;
  4. generated footage, only for what can't be filmed (an idea, a metaphor, the past), and only
     if a generator is already set up.

  With only the first and third, that's a complete edit. Where B-roll would help and nothing
  set up can supply it, leave the person on screen and list the moment with what to find.
- **Real beats generated.** Generated pictures are never shown as evidence that something
  happened, never stand in for real people, products or places as if real, and never carry
  numbers or results nobody said.
- **Moving beats still.** A photo needs gentle motion (a slow push) and stays short; prefer
  footage that moves on its own.
- **Use each clip once.** Another crop or speed of the same shot isn't a new shot.
- **Screens**: crop to the action and make it readable at the size people watch. Never show a
  whole page shrunk down.
- **Keep track** of where every clip came from and its license, and say which are generated.

### 6. Text and captions

- **Captions** are exactly what was said (with names spelled right), timed to the words, one
  group on screen at a time, split where a phrase ends. Size the groups by the pace (section
  2); subtitles are at most 42 characters a line, two lines, and at least 0.7 s on screen.
- **Never cover** a mouth, a face that's talking, or what the viewer needs to read on a screen.
  Make them readable over anything: a box or a strong shadow, checked against what's behind.
- **Titles** stay long enough to read twice (about 1 s plus 0.3 s a word, at least 2 s). A
  person's name goes up when they first matter, not in the first second. One title at a time,
  one style throughout.
- **A graphic is never empty** and shows what the words don't: the evidence, the relationship,
  the example. It doesn't just repeat the caption.

### 7. Sound

- **The voice wins.** Music ducks while people talk and comes up between lines and where nobody
  talks. Land musical hits on cuts and endings, and end the music with the video, not mid-phrase.
- **No dead silence** in the middle: room tone under gaps.
- **Even levels** between people, scenes and clips. Master once, for where it's going: −14 LUFS
  and −1 dBTP for video platforms, −16 LUFS for podcast feeds. Measure the exported file.
- **Sound effects** follow something you can see, sparingly. Not on every cut.
- **Short fades** (10–30 ms) at hard audio cuts, so joins don't click.

#### Music

Music is a choice, not a default. Decide whether the video wants any, then what, then how loud.

- **Whether**: music helps an opening, a montage, a demo, a recap, the space between sections.
  It gets in the way of a dense explanation, a conversation that carries itself, and anything
  sad, serious or sensitive (loss, hard news, a prayer, an apology): leave those without music,
  or ask first. A talk with music only in the intro and outro is often the right answer.
- **What**: match its energy to the content. Calm and steady for long or explanatory videos,
  stories and interviews; more drive for news, demos and recaps; the most for trailers and hype.
  The longer the video, the calmer the music, whatever the topic: past about ten minutes, keep a
  bed low-energy or keep it to the edges. A named track or mood from the person wins.
- **No melody under words.** A lead line or vocals compete with speech. Under dense talking, use
  music that's mostly pads, chords and bass, or the version without its lead; save the melody
  for where nobody talks.
- **How loud**: set it by what's happening, as loudness under the voice (both measured, not
  guessed from a meter):

  | The moment | Music under the voice |
  |---|---|
  | Dense, technical or fast talking | 18–22 dB |
  | Ordinary talking or narration | about 14 dB |
  | Light content where the music is part of the feel | 10–12 dB |
  | An intro, outro or title with little talking | 6–8 dB |
  | Nobody talking (a montage, a transition) | the music leads, about as loud as the voice is elsewhere |

- **Where it comes from**, as with B-roll: music the person handed over or the show already
  uses; then a music library or generator that's already set up; otherwise none, and say where
  it would help. Every track needs a clear license for where the video is going (a monetized
  channel, a podcast feed, a church stream).
- **Adjust by ear.** The person's ear outranks every number here. Turn each note into one change:

  | They say | Change |
  |---|---|
  | "Can't hear the words" / "the music's too loud" | music 4 dB lower |
  | "Can't hear the music" | music 4 dB higher |
  | "The melody's distracting" | a version without the lead, or a track with less melody |
  | "Too busy" | a calmer track, or fewer drums |
  | "Too sleepy" / "too flat" | one step more energy |
  | "Too much music" | keep it to the intro, outro and gaps |

### 8. Check before you hand it over

- Look at the frames on both sides of every cut, and the first seconds frame by frame.
- Listen across every join and to the last word. If you can't hear, say so: a person should.
- Check captions against the words and B-roll against what's being said at that moment.
- Measure the loudness of what you'll deliver.
- Reread your read: does the edit make the point, for those viewers, there?
- Say what you changed, your read and what you assumed, and what a person should check by eye
  or ear.

### 9. Learning from notes

When someone corrects an edit, fix it, then ask whether the note is a rule for next time. If it
is, write it down with the note it came from, when it applies and how to check it. A show's or
channel's own editing notes win over this guide.

When someone wants their videos to feel like other videos (their own past edits, or a channel
they admire), study 3–6 of them before editing. Measure how they're cut: shot length, pauses
kept, where the hook lands, caption density and look, how often B-roll appears, music under
the voice. Write what you find as rules with numbers, ask about anything that looks
deliberate but unclear, and keep it with their notes. Copy how the videos are edited, never
their footage, music or words.
